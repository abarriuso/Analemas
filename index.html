Analemmas

A sundial agrees with mean solar time on only four days a year. The rest of the time the Sun runs up to 16 minutes ahead or 14 behind, and its noon position draws a figure eight. This page computes that curve with a small ephemeris engine and follows the same mechanics to the retrograde loops of the planets and the pentagram of Venus.

01

What an analemma is

An analemma is the closed curve traced by the apparent position of a celestial body when it is observed from a planet's surface at the same mean solar time over a full orbital cycle. The word comes from the Greek ἀνάλημμα ("support", "pedestal"); in antiquity it named a geometric construction for sundials described by Vitruvius, and its modern astronomical sense —the figure-eight curve— only became common in the 18th century (Sawyer, 1994). The physical basis of the curve, the equation of time, was first tabulated rigorously by Flamsteed (1672); its history and modern computation are reviewed in Hughes, Yallop and Hohenkerk (1989).

The resulting shape is the sum of two physical effects acting at once. If both were zero, the analemma would shrink to a point.

Effect I — Orbital eccentricity

Orbits are ellipses with the Sun at one focus (Kepler's 1st law). By the 2nd law (the law of areas), a planet moves faster at perihelion and slower at aphelion. This uneven speed makes the Sun "arrive" late or early compared with a clock that keeps uniform time (Meeus, 1998, pp. 183–185).

Effect II — Axial obliquity

The rotation axis is tilted with respect to the orbital plane (obliquity ε). The tilt makes the projection of the Sun's motion onto the celestial equator uneven through the year and produces the north–south component of the analemma. The contribution Eobl(λ) vanishes at the equinoxes and solstices and reaches its four extremes near λ ≈ 45°, 135°, 225° and 315°, halfway through each season (Müller, 1995; Meeus, 1998, chap. 28).

Analemmas appear on 18th- and 19th-century globes as small figure eights in the Pacific Ocean: they helped navigators and clockmakers convert between solar time and mean time (Sawyer, 1994; Waugh, 1973).

02

Mathematical formalism

The analemma is computed from the equation of time E(t), the difference between the hour angle of the true Sun and that of the fictitious mean Sun. It splits into two physically independent terms (Duffett-Smith, 1990; Hughes et al., 1989; Meeus, 1998, chap. 28):

Equation of time — series expansion
E(t) = Eexc(t) + Eobl(t)
Eexc = −(2e − ¼e³)·sin(M) − 5/4e²·sin(2M) − 13/12e³·sin(3M) + O(e⁴)← eccentricity (= −equation of the centre)
Eobl = tan²(ε/2)·sin(2λ) − ½tan⁴(ε/2)·sin(4λ) + ⅓tan⁶(ε/2)·sin(6λ) + ···← obliquity
e — orbital eccentricity
M — mean anomaly: M = 2πt/T
ε — axial obliquity (rad)
λ — ecliptic longitude of the Sun (rad)
T — anomalistic orbital period (days)
t — time since perihelion (days)

The simulation does not stop at the series. It computes E from its definition, the Greenwich hour angle of the apparent Sun minus mean solar time, with the Earth rotation angle and the IAU 2006 sidereal-time polynomial (Petit & Luzum, 2010); the series is kept to show the two effects apart, and both agree to within one second.

Kepler's equation — Newton-Raphson solution
E − e·sin(E) = M← E = eccentric anomaly; |ΔE| < 10⁻¹²
r = a(1−e²) / (1 + e·cosθ)← heliocentric radius vector
θ = 2 arctan[√((1+e)/(1−e)) · tan(E/2)]← true anomaly
Analemma coordinates (equatorial system)
X axis = αmean(t) − α(t) ≡ E(t)← E > 0: the true Sun transits earlier (it is west at mean noon)
Y axis = δ(t) = arcsin(sinε · sinλ)← geocentric declination

An asymmetric figure eight

When Eobl dominates Eexc, the curve forms a figure eight. Its lobes are unequal because the Sun moves faster near perihelion: on Earth, perihelion (January) falls close to the December solstice, so the southern lobe is the larger one (Müller, 1995).

Other shapes

The shape depends on the relative weight of Eexc (∝ 2e) and Eobl (∝ tan²(ε/2)). On Jupiter (ε ≈ 3.1°, e ≈ 0.048) eccentricity dominates and the curve is an oval with no crossing. On Mars the figure is a teardrop, photographed by the Opportunity rover between 2006 and 2008 (Lakdawalla, 2014). On Mercury the classic analemma cannot even be built: its solar day (~176 Earth days) lasts two of its years (Urschel, n.d.).

How large Earth's equation of time gets

In 2024 the extremes of E(t) were −14 min 12 s (11 February), +3 min 39 s (13 May), −6 min 33 s (25 July) and +16 min 27 s (2 November); they shift by a few seconds from year to year. The components behave differently: Eexc vanishes at perihelion and aphelion and reaches ±7.7 min in early April and early October; Eobl vanishes at the equinoxes and solstices and reaches ±9.9 min around the middle of each season (Hughes et al., 1989; Meeus, 1998, chap. 28; Müller, 1995).

The lower lobe (Nov–Feb) is larger because perihelion falls in the northern winter, when Earth moves fastest. "Sundial noon" is the local mean time at which a sundial shows 12:00; to turn it into clock time, add your offset from the time-zone meridian.

04

Retrograde loops

Seen from Earth, a planet usually drifts eastwards among the stars. Around opposition (the outer planets) or inferior conjunction (Mercury and Venus) it stops, backs westwards for weeks or months and then carries on: a retrograde loop. Nothing happens to the planet; it is the Earth overtaking it, or being overtaken, on the inside lane (Meeus, 1998, chap. 36).

Instead of a figure eight, the five inferior conjunctions of Venus in a ~8-year cycle draw an approximate pentagram on the zodiac (Aveni, 2001). The figure is neither perfect nor closed: it comes from a near orbital resonance (periods: Bretagnon & Simon, 1986; Williams, 2025):

8 : 13 : 5 near orbital resonance
8 tropical years ≈ 2921.94 d
13 orbits of Venus ≈ 2921.11 d
5 synodic periods ≈ 2919.60 d
Mismatch: ~2.3 days in 8 years (0.08 %) → the pentagram drifts ≈ −2.3° per cycle

Each inferior conjunction happens on average ≈ 215.5° further on in ecliptic longitude (≡ 144.5° back): joining them in chronological order gives the {5/2} star, with its five vertices ≈ 72° apart. But the sixth conjunction does not land on the first: it falls ≈ 2.3° behind, so the pentagram does not close and turns a full circle in ≈ 1 250 years. When the cycle completes, the red marker shows the sixth conjunction. Seen from above, the same five dates mark five points of the Earth's orbit, and the Earth–Venus line sweeps out a five-petalled rose.

Venus in ancient astronomy

The oldest surviving systematic observations of Venus come from Mesopotamia: the "Venus tablet of Ammisaduqa" records its heliacal risings and settings and is dated to the 17th century BCE (Hunger & Pingree, 1999). The Venus table of the Maya Dresden Codex (c. 11th–13th century) spans 65 synodic periods —about 104 years— with a canonical value of 584 days and ways of correcting the accumulated error (Aveni, 2001; Bricker & Bricker, 2011). The relation 2920 = 8 × 365 = 5 × 584 days was built into the Maya calendar.

Minor bodies and exoplanets

Pluto (e = 0.249, ε ≈ 122.5°; Williams, 2025) would produce one of the most complex analemmas. On exoplanets with e > 0.3 and arbitrary obliquities, insolation patterns and their analemmas depart radically from the terrestrial case (Dobrovolskis, 2013; Heller et al., 2011).

Method and references

Every figure on this page comes from one file, astro.js, about 350 lines with no dependencies. It turns a date into apparent positions the way an ephemeris does, only with Keplerian elements instead of numerical integration:

The engine is checked on every push by validacion.mjs against reference data downloaded from the NASA/JPL Horizons system (DE441 ephemeris; Park et al., 2021) by tools/fetch-horizons.mjs, and against the test values of the IAU's own libraries. What remains is the error of the Keplerian elements themselves, which ignore mutual perturbations between planets; refraction and the observer's parallax are also left out.
